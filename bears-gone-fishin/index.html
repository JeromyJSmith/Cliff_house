Brett's Gone Fishing

Brett is alone on the skiff, whistling Fishin' Blues. The hold fills up. A small octopus is on his cap, one arm stuck to his cheek. He is the other brain. Brett keeps drifting. A mermaid princess is in the daydream, and somebody has to wake him. One wave lifts the boat, carries it across the country, and splashes it into the seats at Red Rocks. He meets a bear. The crabs follow like ducklings, and one fish flops along on the rock. They lose it, throw full bottles, and hug it out. On the walk down, little bursts of water pop out of the ground behind them. They do not look. He runs the last of the way. Fraser and Rizz are at the door. Rizz is the painter. Two residents are already in the house. They work different shifts. They clock in because the job is to scare people, and they hate it. They are the ones who are scared. A boss is somewhere. They almost never see him. They are nice, and they are smart, they know the physics, and a blank book tells them what to fix. The pages look blank because the writing is the glow, and a sober eye cannot read it. The ghosts stir a pinch of that glow into the drinks so the yard can hear the bear and see the plans. The octopus is usually on Brett's head for that part. The hot tub is perfect until the crabs get stuck, and then the water starts everywhere. It is the old well, lonely and stagnant, trying to come out of the ground. A turkey lives on the property. He gobbles instead of a rooster, in the morning, at supper, and whenever the next thing has no way in. A city inspector comes once, strict past reason, clipboard blank, and he will not follow his own protocol. The bear puts on a sheet. It is a bad costume. The inspector runs and does not come back. Brett understands the first wave was already his. He says he can't leave them with it. They fix the house. Rizz paints the trim and fusses with the curtains. He cooks. They say goodbye to the stone crab and they eat, because the crabs and the fish were not well this far from the Gulf. The blue crab stays. He is the accountant. At the seats, Brett is about to call the water. The octopus squeezes once. He says he almost forgot. The blue crab brings the checkbook. He writes a huge check, the paper blank on purpose, and hands it to Fraser. Then he raises that well into another wave, and the skiff cruises home on it. On the bay the daydream is real. He has a tail, a crooked little crown on the cap, and she is in the boat.

What the story is missing

The opening want is dinner. The true want shows up late: stay, fix the house, leave the money, go home to her. The first wave is one plant. The daydreams are the other. He keeps seeing his future wife, and the people around him keep waking him up, because to them he is smiling at nothing. It pays off twice. He raises the second wave on purpose. Then, on the bay, the tail is real and she is in the boat.

The opposition leaves early. The inspector runs. The boss is never seen. The well is lonely, and it wants to help once it is asked. The feeling has to live in the spoken line, the work, and supper. The check comes after that feeling. It makes the promise solid. The wave takes him home. The king is what the home was for. A check in place of the supper would flatten the story, so the check stays small, and almost forgotten, and the love story gets the last picture.

The theme that holds: things out of place get put back kindly. The Gulf in the mountains. A well that wants out of the ground. Ghosts stuck in the wrong job. A fisherman who can move water, and who was always going to end in it. The check is him leaving the house with more than labor. The crown is him landing where the daydreams were pointing. The book was the missing conversation. It looked blank because a sober page cannot read it. Once the yard shares the state, the bear can be answered and the plans can be followed.

From the street the front is two stories: reddish sandstone, white trim, a pediment, tall windows, an arched door, and a gravel yard. The side wall says Lodging. Morrison History prints 115 Stone Street. The lodge site prints 121. The 2025 photograph is the one the last cartoon follows.

The residents

The published haunting for this house is one newspaper account. On October 12, 2018, the Canyon Courier covered Joel Chirhart's Morrison Ghosts & Legends Tour. The Cliff House Lodge was one of the last stops. Chirhart said former residents have been heard walking through the house at night. A lodge resident passing the tour said he hears loud unexplained banging, and said, "I can't watch scary movies here anymore."

Morrison History does not name a ghost here. George Morrison died in the house in 1895. That is a death record, not a haunting, and the cartoon does not make him either ghost. The tall one is the night shift, the footsteps. The big one is the other shift, the bang. In the cartoon they clock in for a job they hate, which is scaring people, and they are the frightened ones. They are also the brains of the house. They know the physics. The boss is a shadow and an empty chair. He is not drawn with a face, he is not named, and he is not a ghost from any other building. The book they open glows, and the pages look blank. The ghosts stir a pinch of the glow into the drinks and the food so the yard can hear the bear, see the plans, hear the well, and hear the octopus say the math. There is no name and no measure. It is not a recipe. The bear has been talking the whole time, and sober ears hear a bear. None of this is a claim about the lodge. They are nice, and the glow is how they solve the problem in front of them. The page does not borrow the Cliff House at Pikes Peak, the Hatchet Lady at Red Rocks, or the ghosts told at the Holiday Bar, the Morrison Inn, and the Red Rocks Grill. Those are other buildings.
